# SPB Painter Interviews — Pro Advice From Five Archetypes

> Five fictional-but-realistic pro painters sit down for Q&A. Each represents a different school. Read the one that matches your current project, or read all five to stretch your instincts.

---

## Interview 1 — "Ray" — The NASCAR Veteran

> 20 years of door-to-door stock-car livery experience. Works out of a shop in Mooresville with grease-stained radio and a wall of championship photos.

**Q: What's your process for starting a new livery?**
Ray: First thing I do is ask the owner one question — "where is this car going to be photographed?" If it's broadcast TV, the rules are different than a print ad, which is different than a die-cast model. TV eats saturation, so I push colors 15% hotter. Print steals fine lines, so I keep pinstripes at 5mm minimum. Die-cast kills everything below 3mm. If you can't answer that photography question, you're just decorating.

**Q: Biggest mistake you see new painters make?**
Putting the sponsor on every panel. Buddy, a stock car has six panels, and a sponsor on all six means you don't have a sponsor — you have wallpaper. I like *one* hero sponsor, two supporters, and some real estate kept clean for the paint to breathe. Second mistake? Numbers too small. If I can't read the number from the back of the grandstand, you failed. Everything else is second priority.

**Q: Favorite SPB feature and why?**
The spec-per-zone stuff. I can make a car look like it's got a clean gloss on the body and matte on the hood, the way real teams did it for decades because the hood needed anti-glare so the driver didn't get sun-blinded. That kind of period authenticity is new to a paint tool. Nobody else lets me do it in one workflow.

**Q: How long does a pro-level livery take?**
First-run? Four to six hours spread over two days. Why two days? Because the first pass is always a lie. You need to sleep on it. I've thrown out more first-pass designs than most painters have ever made. Sleep on it. Look at it Monday. Keep what still works. Kill what doesn't.

**Q: What's your pre-submission checklist?**
Read the number from 30 feet. Read the primary sponsor from 15 feet. Read the secondary from 5 feet. Look at it in direct sun AND in shadow in the preview. Confirm the design doesn't look awful from the overhead angle, because every broadcast has that camera. Remove any decal that doesn't earn its spot. Sign it, submit it, don't touch it for 24 hours. If you still like it the next day, it was good.

## Interview 2 — "Elena" — The F1 Designer

> Modern, minimalist, lives on airplanes between Milton Keynes and Maranello. Studio walls are whitewashed concrete. Works in vector tools and then paints.

**Q: What's your process for starting a new livery?**
I start with a principle. One written sentence. "This car is about the speed of water." "This car is an apology." "This car is the sound of a door closing." Without a principle, I'm just moving swatches around. Once the principle is locked, every decision is either consistent with it or thrown out. Color, finish, graphic, pattern — all judged against the sentence.

**Q: Biggest mistake you see new painters make?**
They optimize for "looking cool" instead of "looking *intentional*." A cool design with no idea behind it dies in a week. An intentional design — even a modest one — lives forever. The second mistake: starting with graphics and building the body around them. It's backwards. Body first, finish second, graphics last. Graphics should be a whisper after the body has already spoken.

**Q: Favorite SPB feature and why?**
Pattern strength zones. I don't want my carbon to be at 100%. I want it at 38% on one panel, 22% on another, and zero on the third. The weave density is as much a design tool as hue. Most software treats "apply carbon" as a binary switch. SPB treats it like a proper finish parameter. That's why I use it.

**Q: How long does a pro-level livery take?**
For me, eight hours total, but spread across a week. I do a version, walk away two days, come back with fresh eyes. I might run four to six iterations before anything ships. Good design is rewriting, not writing.

**Q: What's your pre-submission checklist?**
Can you describe the car in one sentence to someone who hasn't seen it? If not, the design is confused. Does the design photograph at low resolution and still read? Is there any element I could remove without weakening the car? If yes, remove it. Do the colors make sense under three lighting conditions — sunrise, noon, indoor? If one looks bad, I go back. Only then do I submit.

## Interview 3 — "Takeshi" — The Vintage Specialist

> Restores and recreates historic liveries, working from period photographs. Lives and breathes Group B, Group C, and 60s-70s endurance. Owns more reference books than paint cans.

**Q: What's your process for starting a new livery?**
I start with three to five photographs of the era I'm targeting. Not the car specifically — the *era's aesthetic*. What font did sponsors use? What widths were racing stripes? What reds were actually used in 1973 — because they weren't today's reds. Period reds were less saturated, more brick. Get the *era's vocabulary* down first, then paint the car. Otherwise you'll make a modern car wearing a costume.

**Q: Biggest mistake you see new painters make?**
Modern precision on old cars. A 1972 privateer didn't mask stripes perfectly — they used hand-laid tape, and the stripes were slightly wobbly. They didn't color-match sponsor logos to body paint; sponsors were whatever sponsor decal the guy had in the shop. New painters "clean up" historic designs and destroy the soul. The imperfection is the signature. Respect it.

**Q: Favorite SPB feature and why?**
The damage/wear overlay and subtle wear patterns. Period cars had patina — chips around door handles, dust on lower panels, tape residue from recent re-stripes. SPB lets me dial wear in at 8-15% and suddenly my 1972 privateer looks like it just finished a stage, not like it left a showroom.

**Q: How long does a pro-level livery take?**
For a historic recreation? A week or more of *research*, plus three to four hours of paintwork. The research is 80% of the quality. If you know which shade of burgundy a specific team used, which sponsor decal went on which door, which stripe alignment was correct for which chassis year — the paint job flows in an evening.

**Q: What's your pre-submission checklist?**
Would the original crew recognize this as period-correct? Would a period fan catch anything wrong? Every font, every stripe width, every color saturation checked against photos. Is there a patina layer appropriate to the car's history? Period-correct cars should never look showroom. Last: did I resist the urge to "improve" anything? If I "modernized" anything, I go back and undo it.

## Interview 4 — "Rico" — The Drift Creator

> Loud, bold, graphic-heavy, lives on Instagram. Paints cars that win *crowd noise*, not lap times. Based in Osaka, ships designs globally.

**Q: What's your process for starting a new livery?**
I think about the *moment* — that one photograph the car is going to exist for. Is it a burnout against a sunset? Is it a three-wheel into a hairpin? Is it parked at a car meet at 2am? That moment dictates everything. I don't paint for the driver. I paint for the camera.

**Q: Biggest mistake you see new painters make?**
Being scared. They pick safe colors, safe graphics, safe layouts. Drift livery is not about safety. It's about *statement*. If your car doesn't make somebody laugh out loud the first time they see it, you haven't gone far enough. Second mistake: ignoring the low 30% of the body. That's what the camera catches during slides. Paint the lower panels like they're the front page.

**Q: Favorite SPB feature and why?**
The pattern layering. I love stacking a chevron graphic over a base that already has a horizon gradient. Normal tools fight you when you try to combine like that. SPB lets me layer four design elements on one panel and controls the blend so they actually photograph well. It's a cheat code.

**Q: How long does a pro-level livery take?**
Depends on the car. Quick drift builds? One long afternoon — three hours. Complex multi-graphic showcase cars? Two full days. I iterate a lot — I'll try a graphic, take a screenshot, go to lunch, come back and decide. If I still like it after a meal, it's real.

**Q: What's your pre-submission checklist?**
Does the camera angle for slides make the car look better or worse? I render in 3/4 rear angle specifically. Does the back of the car read? Most drift photos are of the back. Is there enough chaos without crossing into clutter? I remove any graphic that doesn't serve the moment. Last check: show it to three people who don't paint cars. If at least one says "whoa," I ship it.

## Interview 5 — "Nina" — The Minimalist

> Works in a studio with one chair and a north-facing window. Her whole career is *"less is more"* applied to four-wheeled objects.

**Q: What's your process for starting a new livery?**
Subtraction, always. I start by laying down a full, maximalist version — lots of graphics, lots of colors, lots of decals. Then I remove. I take away everything that doesn't earn its place. Usually I end up with 10% of what I started with. The final design is what survives deletion.

**Q: Biggest mistake you see new painters make?**
Adding because they can. The tool gives you a thousand patterns, so they use all of them. The answer isn't "how many features can I use" — it's "what is the *least* I can add and still have a complete design?" Restraint is a skill. Nobody teaches it because it isn't fun. But it's the difference between loud and iconic.

**Q: Favorite SPB feature and why?**
Finish differentiation. I can take a single color and deploy three different finishes across zones — and the car looks designed. No second color needed. No graphics. The finish IS the design. Most tools force you to add color or graphics to feel "complete." SPB lets me finish a design with discipline.

**Q: How long does a pro-level livery take?**
For me, the first version takes 20 minutes. The final version takes 4 hours, because the remaining 3 hours 40 minutes are spent *removing* things. Removal is slower than adding, because every deletion is a judgment call: am I weakening the design, or strengthening it? Only the painter knows.

**Q: What's your pre-submission checklist?**
Can I remove one more thing without losing the identity? If yes, I remove it. Then I ask again. I stop when removing the next element actually weakens the car. That's the minimum viable livery — and usually the best version. Final check: does the car hold up in a silhouette? If I could recognize it from just its outline and finish, I've succeeded.

## Cross-Cutting Advice (From All Five)

- **Sleep on it.** Every painter above mentions at least one form of "walk away."
- **Know your audience.** Ray paints for TV. Elena for galleries. Takeshi for historians. Rico for cameras. Nina for shape. None of them paint for "cool."
- **Subtract before you submit.** Even the loud painters edit ruthlessly.
- **Intentionality beats novelty.** A boring design with a clear thesis beats a flashy design with no reason behind it.
- **Photograph during the build.** Every painter above screenshots at milestones. The photo reveals what the eye misses.
- **Era awareness.** Even the modern painters know their references. History is leverage.
